The Academy of Management Proceedings 2025 contain four contributions with my name on them, all with Juergen Thiesen and Oliver Specht, and one also with Christoph Ihl. Only one of them is mine as first author: a poster in the Research Methods division. The other three are co-authored, two posters and one abstract, with Juergen or Oliver as first author. This post is a short guide to what each one is about.

They cover different ground: AI research, clean technologies, and venture capital twice. What they share is the kind of pipeline behind them. Each starts with a pile of unstructured text that nobody could read by hand (paper abstracts, patents, founder reviews, news articles) and ends with something you can count, cluster or compare. If you have followed this blog for a while, a few of the building blocks will look familiar.

## Attention-based concept mining and community detection in AI research

*Wilinski, Thiesen, Specht. Poster, Research Methods division.* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amproc.2025.23164poster), [PDF](/assets/pdf/aom_poster_2025_jw.pdf)

This is my first-author paper, a poster in the Research Methods division. The motivation goes back to the [AI Concept List](/en/blog/2024-09_ai_concept_list/) I presented at [DRUID 2024](/en/blog/2024-07_post_druid/): bibliometrics can tell you that AI publications and co-authorships are exploding, but they usually cannot tell you *which* ideas travel along those collaborations. Citations show that two papers are related. They do not show who actually picked up "Vision Transformer" first, and from whom.

The paper proposes two layers. The first one is concept mining. We built a database from Papers with Code, roughly 500k AI papers tagged with tasks, methods, datasets, conferences and code repositories, and enriched it with disambiguated authors and full texts from OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar. The result is a knowledge graph with about 513k papers, 380k authors and 1.38 million authorship edges.

![Knowledge graph schema of the AI papers database](/assets/img/blog/aom-2025/knowledge-graph.png)
*The knowledge graph behind the poster. Papers sit in the middle and link to tasks, methods, datasets, repositories, conferences, full texts and authors. The edge counts show where the data is dense (951k task links, 1.38M authorship links) and where it is thin (only 2,085 links from a paper to a method it introduces).*

On top of that we combine three signals to build training data for the concept extractor: a dictionary (tasks, methods and datasets from Papers with Code, the Computer Science Ontology, and the indices of three ML textbooks), frequency (n-grams above a threshold, minus the trivial ones), and attention. The attention part is inspired by UCPhrase: attention maps from a pre-trained transformer tell you whether a candidate phrase behaves like a coherent unit in context. That is what lets the model keep a rare but real concept like "Vision Transformer" or "causal NLP" and throw out junk tokens like "0" or "u". I wrote up the details in [Finding "neural radiance fields" before they are everywhere](/en/blog/2025-02_attention_concept_mining/).

The second layer puts those concepts into yearly co-authorship networks, where nodes are authors and edges are shared papers, and runs community detection (Louvain) on them. Network metrics like density, clustering coefficient and the size of the largest connected component then describe how the field is wired each year, and the concept labels tell you which communities carry which ideas. The observation in the poster is that widely adopted concepts such as transformers tend to start in small clusters before they reshape the wider network. That part has its own post: [Who carries a new idea?](/en/blog/2025-05_coauthorship_networks/).

To be honest about the stage this was at: it is a framework paper. It describes the pipeline and shows the first network snapshots, not a finished causal story about diffusion.

## Measuring novelty using fine-grained concepts: a study in the clean technologies sphere

*Thiesen, Specht, Wilinski. Poster.* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amproc.2025.22096poster)

Juergen's paper attacks a problem that bugs everyone working on novelty indicators. The established measures, Uzzi et al. (2013) on atypical combinations, Foster et al. (2015) and Wang et al. (2017), mostly operate on references or journals: a paper is novel if it cites an unusual combination of journals. That is a proxy at a fairly coarse level. Two papers in the same journal pair can be about completely different things.

The idea here is to compute the same family of measures on fine-grained concepts instead. To get those concepts, the paper builds a hierarchical taxonomy of clean technologies from a domain patent dataset, in three deep learning-based steps: extract meaningful phrases, expand an existing gold-standard concept list, and arrange the result as a taxonomy through hypernym–hyponym relations. The lower, more specific levels of that taxonomy are then the units the novelty measures run on. The comparison the paper sets up is between this taxonomy-based novelty and the conventional reference- or journal-based variants.

If the second step sounds familiar: growing a hand-curated seed list into something much larger is also how the AI Concept List came about, just for a different domain and with patents instead of papers as the text source.

## How founders evaluate VCs: a GPT-based extraction of value-criteria from online VC reviews

*Specht, Wilinski, Thiesen, Ihl. Abstract.* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amproc.2025.18846abstract)

Most research on venture capital decisions looks at the investor side: how VCs select startups, which criteria they apply, how they decide. Oliver's paper flips that around and asks what founders want from their investors.

The data is 8,561 online reviews that founders wrote about VCs. GPT-based text classification pulls 9,229 unique value-criteria out of those reviews, things founders explicitly cared about or complained about. Since 9,229 criteria is not a useful result on its own, a text-embedding clustering step groups them into 26 categories. Combining that with startup lifecycle data then shows which criteria matter at which stage.

The headline findings: "general social skills" of the investor is the most important criterion across all startup stages. More mature startups shift towards more self-serving criteria around growth and long-term relationships. And on the question of whether investors actually deliver, the reviews suggest they mostly do, with "general advice" being the criterion founders see as best fulfilled.

A review is short, messy and opinionated, and the criteria hidden in it are not a fixed list you could match against with a dictionary. That is why the pipeline has two stages: the generative model reads each review and names the criteria in its own words, and the embedding clustering afterwards decides which of those 9,229 phrasings mean the same thing.

## Large scale text analysis for VCs' value-added services: a third-person perspective

*Specht, Wilinski, Thiesen. Poster.* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5465/amproc.2025.21821poster)

VCs like to say they bring more than money. Most evidence for these value-added services comes from the people involved, through surveys of VCs or founders. This paper looks for a third-person view instead, from the news.

The starting corpus is around 30 million news articles. After filtering for relevant content, de-duplication and embedding-based similarity refinement, it comes down to 1.3 million articles with roughly 122 million sentences. That is still far too much to read, so the next step is finding the sentences that actually describe an investor–startup relationship, such as funding announcements and what the startup plans to do next. A fine-tuned DistilBERT classifier does that job, and it beats penalized logistic regression as a baseline. Because a single sentence often loses the context that makes it interpretable, the neighbouring sentences around each hit are put back together.

This one is explicitly work in progress. The abstract names the next step: use larger language models such as GPT-4 to extract structured information from the filtered corpus, so that "investor X helped startup Y with Z" becomes a data point.

## What carries over

Looking at the four contributions side by side, the shared toolkit is pretty clear. Phrase extraction and seed-list expansion show up in the AI and clean-tech papers. Embeddings do the grouping and filtering in both VC papers. And three of the four have a classifier at their core, from an attention-guided concept model to GPT-based classification to a fine-tuned DistilBERT, whose job is to decide which bits of text are worth keeping.

The full text of my contribution is [here as a PDF](/assets/pdf/aom_poster_2025_jw.pdf), and the [publications page](/en/publications/2025-aom-ai-communities/) has the abstract and key points. The three co-authored papers have their own pages too: [novelty in clean tech](/en/publications/2025-aom-novelty-cleantech/), [founders reviewing VCs](/en/publications/2025-aom-vc-reviews/) and [VC value-added services](/en/publications/2025-aom-vc-value-added/).
